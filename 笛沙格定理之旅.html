Desargues' Theorem

Two triangles ABC and A′B′C′ are in perspective from a point S — the lines AA′, BB′, CC′ all pass through S — if and only if they are in perspective from a line: the intersections of corresponding sides, P = AB∩A′B′, Q = BC∩B′C′, R = CA∩C′A′, all lie on one straight line ℓ, the perspective axis (shown in green).

The spatial proof — what you are watching

The scene realizes the two triangles in space: ABC (red) lies in the base plane π, and A′B′C′ (yellow) is the section of the same pyramid S·ABC cut by a plane π′, so AA′, BB′, CC′ automatically concur at the apex S.

  1. AB and A′B′ both lie in the plane SAB, so they meet at a point P (unless parallel).
  2. P lies on AB ⊂ π and on A′B′ ⊂ π′, hence on ℓ = π∩π′.
  3. The same argument gives Q, R ∈ ℓ — so P, Q, R are collinear. The green axis is nothing but the intersection line of the two planes.

Tilt the cutting plane or press ▶ Animate: P, Q, R slide along, but never leave the axis. The 📽️ Project button flattens the whole spatial configuration onto the paper plane σ — that is the planar picture of the theorem, with collinearity preserved.

Why 3D beats 2D

In space there is almost nothing to do: the axis ℓ = π∩π′ exists for free, and each pair of corresponding sides is forced to meet on it because both are trapped in a single plane through S. The whole proof is three short sentences. A purely planar proof receives no such gift — you must manufacture auxiliary points and chase length ratios (classically via Menelaus' theorem applied several times), which is long and delicate.

The catch: the embedding is assumed, not guaranteed

The spatial argument proves the theorem only for configurations that can be embedded in 3D — two triangles realized as sections of one and the same pyramid. Given an abstract plane with a perspective configuration drawn in it, nothing in this proof guarantees that the picture can actually be lifted into such a spatial structure. That liftability is an extra structural assumption on the plane, essentially equivalent to Desargues' theorem itself: planes where it fails (non-Desarguesian planes) genuinely exist, and there the theorem is simply false. The short proof buys its simplicity by presupposing the very embedding structure it relies on.
